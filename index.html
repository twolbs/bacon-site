Breakfast

30 min • Serves 4

Maple & black pepper bacon

The weekend classic, with a sweet little twist.

Get the recipe →

Ingredients

  • 450 g thick-cut bacon
  • 2 tbsp pure maple syrup
  • 1 tsp freshly cracked black pepper

Method

  1. Heat the oven to 200°C / 400°F. Line a rimmed baking sheet with foil and set an oven-safe wire rack on top.
  2. Arrange bacon in one layer on the rack. Bake for 15 minutes, then carefully brush with maple syrup and sprinkle with pepper.
  3. Bake 5–10 minutes more, checking often, until browned and cooked through. Timing varies with thickness.
  4. Rest for 3 minutes before serving; hot syrup can burn. Let the tray cool before handling the rendered fat.
Lunch

20 min • Serves 2

The really good BLT

Ripe tomatoes. Crisp bacon. Absolutely no shortcuts.

Get the recipe →

Ingredients

  • 6 slices bacon
  • 4 slices sourdough bread
  • 1 large ripe tomato, sliced
  • 4 leaves butter lettuce
  • 2 tbsp mayonnaise
  • Black pepper, to taste

Method

  1. Start bacon in a cold skillet and cook over medium-low heat for 8–12 minutes, turning often, until browned and cooked through. Transfer to a paper-lined plate.
  2. Toast bread. Pat tomato slices dry and season with black pepper.
  3. Spread mayonnaise on the toast. Layer lettuce, tomato and bacon between two slices.
  4. Cut in half and serve immediately. Makes 2 sandwiches.
Dinner

35 min • Serves 3

Smoky bacon & leek pasta

A cozy, silky supper from a handful of ingredients.

Get the recipe →

Ingredients

  • 300 g dried pasta
  • 150 g bacon, chopped
  • 2 leeks, washed and thinly sliced
  • 100 ml heavy cream
  • 40 g Parmesan, grated
  • 1 tbsp olive oil
  • Black pepper and lemon juice

Method

  1. Bring a pot of water to a boil. Salt lightly, cook pasta until al dente, and reserve 250 ml cooking water before draining.
  2. Cook bacon in a large skillet over medium heat for 8–10 minutes until cooked through. Remove bacon, leaving 1 tbsp fat in the pan.
  3. Add leeks and olive oil if needed. Cook gently for 8–10 minutes until soft. Stir in cream and 100 ml pasta water; simmer for 2 minutes.
  4. Toss in pasta and bacon. Remove from heat and stir in Parmesan. Add more pasta water to loosen, then finish with pepper and a little lemon juice. Serves 3.

A STORY CENTURIES IN THE MAKING

Before the breakfast.
There was the craft.

Long before refrigeration, salting and drying helped people preserve pork. What began as a practical tradition became a world of regional flavors.

Bacon usually means cured pork, often smoked. But the cut, cure and meaning change from one kitchen to the next.

01 / PRESERVATION

Salt came first.

Salted pork has deep roots in many food cultures, including ancient China and Europe. These were ancestors of today’s bacon, rather than one single invention.

02 / LOCAL TRADITIONS

A taste of a place.

Dry curing, brining and smoking evolved around climate, available wood and local tastes. British back bacon and American belly bacon tell different parts of that story.

03 / THE MODERN TABLE

Beyond breakfast.

Refrigeration and commercial production made bacon widely available. Today, small producers and home cooks explore heritage breeds, wood smoke and thoughtful sourcing.

KNOW YOUR BACON

Better bacon starts with understanding.

The breed brings character.

Breed is only part of the story: feed, husbandry, age and curing also shape flavor and texture.

Berkshire

A heritage breed associated with well-marbled pork and a rich eating quality.

Tamworth

A long-bodied heritage breed traditionally valued for bacon production.

Duroc

Often selected for growth and meat quality; its pork can offer good marbling.

Large White / Yorkshire

A widely raised breed often found in commercial crosses, with a relatively lean carcass.

THE BUTCHER’S NOTES

Same animal. Different slice.

01 Belly → streaky bacon
Distinct ribbons of fat and meat. Ideal for crisp rashers, wrapping and lardons.
02 Loin → back bacon
A leaner eye of meat, sometimes with a belly tail. A British and Irish breakfast favorite.
03 Shoulder → cottage bacon
A meatier, often rounded slice. Naming varies by butcher and region.
04 Jowl → jowl bacon
A rich, fatty cut. Italian guanciale is cured jowl, typically unsmoked, with its own traditions.

“Uncured” bacon may still use nitrate or nitrite sources such as celery powder. Read the label rather than relying on the name.

ONE INGREDIENT. MANY TRADITIONS.

Around the world in rashers.

A different cut, a different cure.
Always a sense of place.

01 / UNITED KINGDOM & IRELAND

The breakfast rasher

Back bacon, usually cut from the loin with some belly attached, takes its place alongside eggs, tomatoes and other regional breakfast staples.

02 / UNITED STATES

Smoke & streaks

Smoked pork belly is the familiar American style. Hickory and applewood offer different smoke profiles, while thick cuts suit oven cooking.

03 / ITALY

The art of the cure

Pancetta is cured belly, often rolled or flat and usually unsmoked. Guanciale comes from the jowl; both build savory depth in pasta and beans.

04 / GERMANY & AUSTRIA

A little speck

Speck describes different cured pork products by region. Tyrolean styles often combine gentle smoke and air drying; cuts and serving customs vary.

A note on names: these cured pork traditions are related, but they are not interchangeable. Follow the cooking directions on the package.

A FEW GOOD KITCHEN HABITS

Keep raw bacon refrigerated at 4°C / 40°F or below and follow the package’s storage and cooking instructions. Wash hands and surfaces after handling raw pork. Refrigerate cooked leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3–4 days. Let fat cool before disposal; never pour it down the sink.
